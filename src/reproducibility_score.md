# Reproducibility index in `consistency_analysis.ipynb`

The composite is built per profile in three layers — a **structural** score over the workflow graph, a **content** score over the LLM's per-node reproducibility ratings, and a **geometric mean** combining the two.

---

## 1. Structural score `S` (cell `ca-41`, `structural_score`)

$$
S \;=\; 0.25\,r_{\text{src}} \;+\; 0.25\,r_{\text{sink}} \;+\; 0.20\,r_{\text{in}} \;+\; 0.15\,r_{\text{reach}} \;+\; 0.15\,r_{\text{lwcc}}
$$

Term-by-term:

- $S$ — composite structural score for one profile, $S \in [0, 1]$.
- $0.25,\ 0.25,\ 0.20,\ 0.15,\ 0.15$ — fixed weights summing to 1; sources and sinks dominate because if a declared dataset is never read or a declared figure is never produced, the workflow is incomplete regardless of internal wiring quality.
- $r_{\text{src}}, r_{\text{sink}}$ — boundary connectivity (do declared sources/sinks actually connect to the rest of the graph?).
- $r_{\text{in}}$ — local input hygiene (do process input labels resolve to known nodes?).
- $r_{\text{reach}}, r_{\text{lwcc}}$ — global topology (does the graph form one connected pipeline from input to output?).

The graph is $G = (\text{sources} \cup \text{processes} \cup \text{sinks},\ \text{edges})$ built in `build_graph`: a source→process edge is added when a source ID appears in a process's `input_ids`; process→process when outputs match a downstream `input_ids`; process→sink when a sink ID appears in `outcomes`.

---

### `sources_consumed_ratio` $r_{\text{src}}$

$$
r_{\text{src}} \;=\; \frac{\#\{s \in \text{sources} : \deg^{+}(s) > 0\}}{\lvert\text{sources}\rvert}
$$

- $\text{sources}$ — set of source nodes (datasets) declared in `nodes_source`.
- $\deg^{+}(s)$ — out-degree of source $s$, i.e. number of edges leaving $s$ (each edge = a process that consumes $s$).
- $\deg^{+}(s) > 0$ — the source feeds at least one process.
- $\#\{\cdot\}$ — count of elements satisfying the predicate.
- $\lvert\text{sources}\rvert$ — total number of declared sources.
- **Meaning**: fraction of declared input datasets that are actually wired into the methodology. Penalises sources nobody reads.
- **References**: standard out-degree definition in graph theory — Diestel (2017), *Graph Theory*, ch. 1; analogous to "input coverage" in data-flow analysis (Aho, Sethi, Ullman, *Compilers: Principles, Techniques, and Tools*, ch. 9).

---

### `sinks_produced_ratio` $r_{\text{sink}}$

$$
r_{\text{sink}} \;=\; \frac{\#\{t \in \text{sinks} : \deg^{-}(t) > 0\}}{\lvert\text{sinks}\rvert}
$$

- $\text{sinks}$ — set of sink nodes (figures, tables, reported metrics) declared in `nodes_sink`.
- $\deg^{-}(t)$ — in-degree of sink $t$, i.e. number of edges entering $t$ (each edge = a process that produces $t$).
- $\deg^{-}(t) > 0$ — the sink is produced by at least one process.
- **Meaning**: fraction of figures/tables that are actually generated by some process. Penalises orphan sinks (a claimed result with no method that produces it).
- **References**: dual of $r_{\text{src}}$; mirrors the "covered output" notion in workflow-completeness audits (Goble et al., *FAIR Computational Workflows*, Data Intelligence 2020).

---

### `resolved_input_ratio` $r_{\text{in}}$

$$
r_{\text{in}} \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\#\text{dangling inputs}}{\#\text{total process inputs}}
$$

- $\text{total process inputs}$ — $\sum_{p \in \text{processes}}\lvert\text{input\_ids}(p)\rvert$, the total number of input slots across every process node.
- *dangling input* — an input label whose string is not in $\text{sources} \cup \text{sinks} \cup \bigcup_{p}\text{outcomes}(p)$, i.e. it does not match any declared node ID anywhere in the profile.
- $\#\text{dangling inputs}$ — count of dangling input slots, summed across all processes.
- $1 - (\cdot)$ — converts a fraction-bad into a fraction-good so higher is better, matching the other terms.
- **Meaning**: share of process inputs that resolve to a known node. Penalises unresolved IDs, typos, and references to non-existent artefacts — these would break a real pipeline.
- **References**: same idea as "use-before-def" / dangling-reference checks in static program analysis (Nielson, Nielson, Hankin, *Principles of Program Analysis*, 1999); also analogous to the "shape-graph soundness" predicate in dataflow validation (Sagiv, Reps, Wilhelm, TOPLAS 2002).

---

### `source_to_sink_reachability` $r_{\text{reach}}$

$$
r_{\text{reach}} \;=\; \frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert\,\lvert T\rvert}\sum_{s \in S}\sum_{t \in T}\mathbb{1}[s \rightsquigarrow t]
$$

- $S, T$ — alias for the source set and the sink set in this expression.
- $\lvert S\rvert\,\lvert T\rvert$ — total number of (source, sink) pairs; the normaliser.
- $s \rightsquigarrow t$ — there exists a directed path from $s$ to $t$ in $G$ (computed via `nx.has_path`).
- $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ — indicator function: $1$ if the predicate holds, $0$ otherwise.
- The double sum counts the connected pairs.
- **Meaning**: probability that a random (source, sink) pair is connected by some directed path. Penalises broken pipelines where you cannot trace a path from raw data to a published figure.
- **References**: directed reachability / transitive-closure problem — Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, §22.5; widely used as a workflow-integrity check in scientific-workflow systems (Davidson & Freire, SIGMOD 2008, "Provenance and scientific workflows").

---

### `lwcc_fraction` $r_{\text{lwcc}}$

$$
r_{\text{lwcc}} \;=\; \frac{\lvert\text{LWCC}(G)\rvert}{\lvert V\rvert}
$$

- $V$ — node set of $G$ (every source, process, sink).
- *weakly connected component (WCC)* — a maximal set of nodes that are mutually reachable when edge directions are ignored.
- $\text{LWCC}(G)$ — the largest such component (computed via `nx.weakly_connected_components`).
- $\lvert\text{LWCC}\rvert / \lvert V\rvert$ — share of nodes in the dominant blob.
- **Meaning**: how concentrated the workflow is in one connected piece. Penalises stray disconnected fragments (a process that references nothing, a sink with its own isolated mini-graph). Equals $1$ when the whole profile is one connected piece.
- **References**: weakly connected component definition — Tarjan (1972), *Depth-first search and linear graph algorithms*, SIAM J. Comput.; LWCC fraction is a standard "giant component" measure in network science (Newman, *Networks*, 2nd ed., §6.12).

---

The five weights `(0.25, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.15)` sum to 1, so $S \in [0, 1]$.

#### Why the structural score normalises to $[0, 1]$

The denominator inside each term is **the maximum that term could attain for this profile**, not a constant across profiles. So each term is the *fraction* of an opportunity taken:

- $r_{\text{src}}$: numerator = sources actually consumed; denominator $\lvert\text{sources}\rvert$ = sources that *could* be consumed in this profile. Bounded in $[0, 1]$ by construction (you can't consume more sources than were declared).
- $r_{\text{sink}}$: same shape, with sinks.
- $r_{\text{in}}$: numerator $\#\text{dangling}$ is at most $\#\text{total process inputs}$ (the denominator), so the ratio is in $[0, 1]$ and $1 - (\cdot)$ is too.
- $r_{\text{reach}}$: indicator sums lie in $[0, \lvert S\rvert\,\lvert T\rvert]$, dividing by $\lvert S\rvert\,\lvert T\rvert$ gives $[0, 1]$.
- $r_{\text{lwcc}}$: the LWCC is a subset of $V$, so $\lvert\text{LWCC}\rvert / \lvert V\rvert \in [0, 1]$.

Each $r_{\bullet} \in [0, 1]$, and the convex combination $S = \sum_i w_i r_i$ with $\sum_i w_i = 1$ stays in $[0, 1]$. The weights normalise the *layers against each other* (so sources count for 25% of the total, etc.); the per-term denominators normalise *within each term* (so a profile with 3 sources and a profile with 30 sources are both scored on the same $[0, 1]$ scale). The two normalisations are independent — that's why the formula always lands in $[0, 1]$ regardless of profile size.

A degenerate case worth knowing: if a profile declares no sources, $r_{\text{src}}$ is undefined ($0/0$) and the code falls back to $0$ (or skips the term). The same applies to $r_{\text{sink}}$ and $r_{\text{reach}}$ when either side is empty. That is a deliberate "no evidence = no credit" choice, not a normalisation artefact.

## 2. Content score `C` (cell `ca-41`, `content_score`)

Each node carries an LLM-assigned `reproducibility_score ∈ [0, 100]`. For each layer $\ell \in \{\text{source}, \text{process}, \text{sink}\}$ present in the profile,

$$
\bar{c}_{\ell} \;=\; \frac{1}{100}\cdot\frac{1}{\lvert\ell\rvert}\sum_{n \in \ell} \text{reproducibility\_score}(n) \;\in\; [0,1]
$$

Term-by-term:

- $\ell$ — one of the three layers (source / process / sink).
- $\lvert\ell\rvert$ — number of nodes in that layer for this profile.
- $n \in \ell$ — a node in the layer.
- $\text{reproducibility\_score}(n)$ — the integer rubric score the analyst assigned to node $n$, on $[0, 100]$.
- $\frac{1}{\lvert\ell\rvert}\sum$ — arithmetic mean across nodes in the layer.
- $\frac{1}{100}\cdot$ — rescales from $[0,100]$ to $[0,1]$ so it is commensurate with $S$.

With layer weights $w_{\text{src}}=0.30,\ w_{\text{proc}}=0.40,\ w_{\text{sink}}=0.30$, and re-normalising over only the **present** layers $P$:

$$
C \;=\; \sum_{\ell \in P} \frac{w_{\ell}}{\sum_{k \in P} w_{k}}\;\bar{c}_{\ell}
$$

Term-by-term:

- $P \subseteq \{\text{source}, \text{process}, \text{sink}\}$ — set of layers present (i.e. non-empty) in the profile.
- $w_{\ell}$ — fixed weight for layer $\ell$.
- $\sum_{k \in P} w_{k}$ — total weight of present layers; dividing by it re-normalises so the effective weights sum to 1 even if a layer is missing.
- $\bar{c}_{\ell}$ — that layer's mean rubric score (above).
- The outer sum is the weighted average across layers.

Re-normalisation keeps $C \in [0, 1]$ when a layer is missing (e.g. a profile with no sinks). Process gets the largest weight (0.40) because methodology drives reproducibility more than the data list or the figure list.

**References**: the *categorisation* into source / process / sink layers is loosely inspired by the grouping in the NeurIPS 2019 ML Reproducibility Checklist (Pineau et al., 2021, *J. Mach. Learn. Res.*, §5 and Appendix Fig. 8), which organises items into "models and algorithms", "theoretical claims", and "figures and tables that present empirical results". Note that Pineau et al. deliberately do **not** define a composite score — they explicitly state that reproducibility findings "[do not] lend themselves to such a coarse summary" (§4, p. 10). The weighted-mean aggregation across layers and the re-normalisation over present layers are our own construction — to our knowledge there is no published reproducibility rubric that uses this exact aggregator. Cochrane meta-analysis (Higgins et al., *Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions*, §10) and ACM's categorical Artifact Review and Badging system (ACM, v1.1, 2020) are sometimes invoked as related work, but they answer different questions (pooling effect-size estimates across studies via inverse-variance weighting; assigning discrete badges with $\{-1, 0, +1\}$ per-badge scores) and do not provide a precedent for the convex combination defined here.

## 3. Composite `repro_index` (cell `ca-42`)

$$
\text{repro\_index} \;=\; \sqrt{\max(S,0)\cdot\max(C,0)}
$$

Term-by-term:

- $S, C$ — the structural and content scores defined above, each in $[0,1]$.
- $\max(\cdot, 0)$ — clamps to non-negative before the square root, defensively guarding against any future term that could go negative.
- $\sqrt{S \cdot C}$ — the **geometric mean** of the two scores. Equals the side of a square whose area is $S \cdot C$.
- The result is in $[0, 1]$, with $1$ achieved only when both $S = 1$ and $C = 1$.

The geometric mean is deliberate: it punishes lopsided profiles. A graph that is perfectly wired ($S=1$) but whose nodes are barely described ($C \approx 0.2$) gets $\sqrt{0.2}\approx 0.45$, not $0.6$. Both halves have to be good for the index to be good — structural plumbing without content, or content without plumbing, are each insufficient evidence of reproducibility.

**References**: the geometric mean is used here as a **non-compensatory** aggregator — a profile with $S = 1, C \approx 0$ (or vice versa) should not be allowed to score near $0.5$, since one half of the reproducibility evidence is effectively absent. The same construction and the same rationale underlie the UNDP Human Development Index (UNDP, *Human Development Report 2010 Technical Notes*), where life expectancy, education, and income indices are aggregated via $(I_{\text{life}} \cdot I_{\text{edu}} \cdot I_{\text{inc}})^{1/3}$ rather than an arithmetic mean, precisely so that a deficit in one dimension cannot be masked by surplus in another. Ebert & Welsch (2004), *Meaningful environmental indices: a social choice approach*, *J. Environmental Economics and Management*, give a measurement-theoretic argument for the geometric mean when sub-metrics live on independent ratio scales; that argument applies less directly here (both $S$ and $C$ are already dimensionless and bounded in $[0, 1]$), but their result is consistent with the choice.

## 4. Aggregation across runs (cells `ca-51`–`ca-54`)

For each `(model, temperature)` cell the notebook reports `mean ± std` of $S$, $C$, and `repro_index` across the 10 sample runs:

$$
\hat{\mu}_{m,T} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{m,T,i},\qquad
\hat{\sigma}_{m,T} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(x_{m,T,i}-\hat{\mu}_{m,T}\bigr)^2}
$$

Term-by-term:

- $m, T$ — model and temperature defining the cell.
- $N$ — number of samples per cell ($N = 10$ in this corpus).
- $x_{m,T,i}$ — score of the $i$-th sample; here $x \in \{S, C, \text{repro\_index}\}$.
- $\hat{\mu}_{m,T}$ — sample mean (point estimate of the cell's score).
- $\hat{\sigma}_{m,T}$ — sample standard deviation with $N-1$ in the denominator (Bessel-corrected, the pandas default for `.std`); measures run-to-run consistency at fixed prompt and temperature.

So the final reported number for `repro_index` is the **arithmetic mean of per-run geometric means**: the mean averages across runs, while each run's geometric mean penalises imbalance within that run. The std at a fixed $(m, T)$ is the empirical proxy for "consistency" — a model with low $\hat{\sigma}$ at $T=0$ but high $\hat{\sigma}$ at $T=2$ is reproducible only at low temperature.

**References**: sample mean and Bessel-corrected sample variance — Casella & Berger (2002), *Statistical Inference*, 2nd ed., §5.2; the use of run-to-run variance as a stochastic-LLM consistency metric mirrors the protocol in Ouyang et al. (2025), "Empirical study on LLM-based test-case generation" (consistency = within-temperature variance).
